What your portfolio pays you, and what your life costs

Two halves of the same question. The first tab turns balances into after-tax spendable income and projects it across your whole retirement, with inflation. The second builds the budget it has to cover — including health care, which is the expense that actually decides whether retiring early works. Tax-deferred accounts are left untouched until 59½.

How the income side works. Spendable income is every dollar of cash you receive — portfolio withdrawal, Social Security, pension — minus anything you contribute to a 401(k) or Roth IRA and minus the year's total tax bill. Withdrawals are sourced in order: taxable brokerage first, then tax-deferred once you are 59½, then Roth. Dividends and interest are taxed as they arrive. Price appreciation is not taxed until you sell, and when you do, only the gain portion of the sale is taxable. Tax-deferred withdrawals are ordinary income, Roth withdrawals are untaxed, and Social Security is taxed on the provisional-income test, which makes between 0% and 85% of the benefit taxable. The standard deduction is applied to ordinary income first, then qualified dividends and long-term gains stack on top to find their 0/15/20% rate.

  • No early withdrawals, but forced ones later. Tax-deferred balances are untouched before 59½, so no 10% penalty ever applies and nothing is modelled for 72(t) schedules, the rule of 55, or Roth conversion ladders. Before 59½ the Roth is the fallback if the brokerage runs dry, which assumes what comes out is contribution basis rather than earnings; earnings withdrawn early would be taxed and penalised. From the required-distribution age — 73, or 75 if you were born in 1960 or later — the IRS Uniform Lifetime Table overrides the withdrawal order and forces money out as ordinary income whether you want it or not. Leaving a 401(k) alone for decades makes that forced distribution large, and this is where a long plan's tax bill actually lands. Cash you receive but do not spend — whether from a forced distribution or simply from drawing more than your budget needs — is reinvested in the taxable brokerage at full cost basis, so a required distribution is a taxable transfer between accounts rather than money lost.
  • Social Security. Reduced by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months claimed before a full retirement age of 67, and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond that — a 30% cut at 62 — or increased by 8% a year for delay past 67. The benefit is stated in today's dollars and grown by the COLA. The provisional-income thresholds that decide how much of it is taxable ($25,000/$34,000 single, $32,000/$44,000 joint) are fixed in statute and never indexed, so a rising share of the benefit becomes taxable over a long retirement. Spousal and survivor benefits and the earnings test are not modelled.
  • Health care before 65 is priced off the 2026 ACA benchmark silver plan, about $1,060 a month at age 60 unsubsidised, scaled by the federal default age curve. The premium tax credit uses the 2026 rules with the enhanced subsidies expired, so the 400%-of-poverty subsidy cliff is back: at $62,000 of income a 60-year-old pays about $515 a month, and $2,000 more income costs the entire credit. The credit has a floor as well as a ceiling: below 100% of the poverty line there is none either, because coverage is meant to come from Medicaid — or from nothing at all in the ten states that did not expand it. So the credit exists only inside a corridor, and both walls are modelled. Because your AGI drives this, realising capital gains raises your premium as well as your tax, and the withdrawal table prices each draw rate's health care separately for exactly that reason. Marketplace premiums vary widely by county and plan; one national benchmark is used here.
  • Health care from 65 is Medicare: the 2026 Part B premium of $202.90, a typical Part D premium, a Medigap plan, and out-of-pocket spending. IRMAA surcharges are applied from the 2026 brackets, which begin at $109,000 single and $218,000 joint. In reality IRMAA looks at your income from two years earlier; it is applied to the current year here, so a one-off spike shows up a year or two early. Long-term care, which Medicare largely does not cover, is not included — add it in the additional health spending field.
  • Cost of living figures are broad-brush indices for a single adult, scaled by 1.15 on housing and 1.6 on everything else for a two-person household, then by your lifestyle multiplier. They are order-of-magnitude guidance for comparing places, not quotes: rents inside one city vary more than the gap between many of these cities. Health costs abroad are typical private international or local cover for someone in their late fifties and rise sharply with age. Small beach and surf towns behave differently from their country's average — housing carries a foreigner and ocean-view premium while local food and labour stay cheap, so the housing and non-housing indices diverge much more than they do in a capital city. Places flagged as having no nearby hospital are flagged for a reason: air ambulance cover is a separate purchase, and residency in most of these countries requires a visa with its own income test.
  • 2026 federal brackets, standard deductions, and long-term gain thresholds, including the additional deduction at 65 and the $6,000 senior deduction with its 6% phaseout. That senior deduction is scheduled to expire after 2028, which this does not model. Contribution limits are the 2026 $24,500 elective deferral and $7,500 IRA limits plus catch-ups from 50, with the Roth phaseout applied as a straight line rather than the IRS rounding rules. Brackets, deductions, limits and poverty guidelines are indexed to your general inflation rate; NIIT and Social Security thresholds are not, because statute fixes them.
  • Local tax follows the location you pick. Each place carries one effective rate as a stand-in for real brackets, exemptions and retirement-income exclusions, plus whether it taxes Social Security and whether it exempts tax-deferred withdrawals — Pennsylvania, Illinois and Mississippi do, almost nowhere else. Override the rate and it stops following the location. Abroad, the rate is what a resident US retiree would face locally, and the territorial countries are set to zero because they do not tax foreign-source income at all, which is what a US portfolio is to them. A US citizen owes US federal tax everywhere on earth, so the treaty setting decides whether the local bill lands on top of the federal one or credits against it; reality usually sits between those two, and because much US-source investment income cannot be resourced under a treaty, double taxation is a real risk. Wealth taxes are not modelled as such — the Netherlands taxes a deemed return on assets rather than income, which on a large portfolio behaves nothing like the rate shown. Treat all of it as a prompt to get local advice, not as an answer.
  • Sequence-of-returns risk is modelled, but only as a model. Every table except the stress test assumes the same return every year, which is the most flattering assumption on this page. The stress panel keeps your average return and moves only when the bad years land; the paired scenarios are the point, because an identical decade early and late produce very different endings. The thousand random runs draw each year independently from a normal distribution, which is kinder than reality — real markets have fatter tails and crashes cluster — so read that success rate as an optimistic bound rather than a probability. It is not historical data and makes no claim to be.
  • Still absent: itemized deductions, credits, QBI, AMT, tax-loss harvesting (which matters more than it sounds — a long downturn can leave a large unrealised loss earning nothing here), employer matching, backdoor Roth conversions, and Roth conversion ladders. Cash you fall short by is simply reported as a shortfall rather than funded by selling more, so a plan that runs short keeps its portfolio intact on paper when in reality you would be spending it down faster.

This is an estimating tool, not tax or investment advice. Run real numbers with a CPA or a licensed advisor before making decisions that depend on them.
